How this site works

An independent index of hardware security tools. No affiliate links, no sponsorships, no vendor review copies. The point is to be the thing you wish existed before you spent $200 on something that turned out to be an ESP32 in a nice case.

Confidence, not claims

Most spec sheets in this space are marketing. A vendor writes "5 GHz Wi-Fi attacks" because the chip they used supports 5 GHz, not because their firmware implements it. So every capability here carries a confidence level rather than a tick:

Claimed is the important one. It means the seller says it does this and we could not find any independent confirmation. It is not an accusation, but treat it as a question to ask before you buy.

The honest take

Every device has a field that exists purely to say the thing the seller will not. Whether the headline "detector" is really a Bluetooth scan matching a list of known identifiers. Whether the $200 handheld runs the same free firmware you could flash onto a $15 board. Whether the project was last updated in 2021. Whether the attack it advertises still works against anything modern.

Firmware is listed separately, on purpose

A Flipper Zero running stock firmware and the same Flipper running a community build are genuinely different products in daily use. So they get separate entries that share a hardware family, and each device page links to its siblings. The same applies to a cheap board running one of the open-source firmware projects: the board is a commodity, the firmware is the product.

The firmware is free, the hardware is not

The single most useful thing this index does is notice when the same open-source firmware runs on both a cheap board and an expensive handheld. The browse page lists the biggest of these gaps, and every device page shows cheaper hardware running identical software.

This is not an accusation of overcharging. A case, a battery, a screen, an antenna connector and a better radio are real engineering and real cost, and for many people they are worth paying for. But you should know which part of the price is capability and which part is convenience, because the sellers rarely volunteer it.

Why some things are marked restricted

There is a hard line between owning a tool and using it. Owning nearly everything indexed here is lawful in most countries. Transmitting on licensed bands, jamming, deauthenticating networks, cloning credentials, and injecting keystrokes into machines you do not own are separate criminal offences almost everywhere, and the device being legal to buy is not a defence.

Capabilities tagged restricted are ones where the act of using the feature is typically an offence unless you own the target or hold written authorisation. caution means it is usually lawful to do passively but can cross a line depending on where you are and what you do with the data.

Per-device legal notes flag known import and sale restrictions. Those notes are desk research, not legal advice and not a review of primary legislation. Law in this area changes quickly. Check your own jurisdiction.

Prices

Prices are manufacturer-direct in US dollars and carry the date they were checked. Where a project is build-it-yourself, the figure is an estimated parts cost and is marked as such. Several devices here are made in tiny batches by one person and are frequently out of stock; street prices can be far above list.

What is deliberately not here

Monitor-mode Wi-Fi adapters as standalone products, automotive and CAN bus tools, drone hardware, and physical entry tools. Each is a large field that deserves better than a footnote.

Corrections

The data lives in one file, data/devices.js, with a documented schema. If something here is wrong, it is wrong in a way that is easy to fix, and being told is genuinely welcome. Fields marked "what we could not confirm" on each device page are open questions.
